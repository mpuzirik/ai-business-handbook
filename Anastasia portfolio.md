# Portfolio Entry — Week 1

During this week I collaborated with my team to establish the focus of our project concerning AI development in businesses. One of the key decisions we made relates to working with a particular SME category rather than trying to cover SMEs as a whole. Our choice was to investigate small marketing and advertising companies, specifically focusing on **Reklaame**, a company from Arnhem.

I assisted in the definition of the general business problem we should consider. Rather than examining the types of AI technologies that marketing agencies might be using, we decided to ask ourselves the important question of how generative AI can be used to enhance productivity while keeping creativity, originality and trust in clients. I believe that this distinction is worth mentioning because AI deployment has to be oriented toward business outcome instead of following the latest technology trends.

I also worked on drafting our research proposal **AIBS** and the product requirements document **AEL**. This gave me a more complete understanding of the interconnections between both modules. In AIBS, I defined what we should conduct research on, whereas, in AEL we focused on what platform we will need in order to gather, assess, convert all the information into the handbook.

One significant aspect I noted during the work process was that we do not have to presume that we know the issues of the selected SME. For instance, we believe that AI might help save time on various tasks – from researching and brainstorming to drafting content and managing client briefs, but these are only presumptions. We need to validate them with the help of desk research and SME visit.

My next task will be to find appropriate sources in Dutch and English on the topic of the adoption of AI in marketing SMEs and gather information necessary for making contacts and interviewing a professional agency. AEL workflow simple enough that a non-technical team member can use it.
# Portfolio Entry — Week 2

This week, our team worked on the second stage of the AI in Business project. We researched the geopolitical AI landscape, particularly the positions of the United States, China and Europe. Our handbook work focused on translating this broad topic into something relevant for our target audience: small marketing and advertising agencies in the Netherlands. Instead of simply asking which country dominates AI, we focused on understanding where the AI technologies businesses depend on come from and what risks these dependencies can create.

My main contribution this week was to the research and comparison of different perspectives on global AI leadership. I worked with AI-generated articles about the United States, China and Europe and compared their conclusions. I noticed that the articles often sounded equally confident even when they focused on different measurements or presented different interpretations of AI leadership.

I also contributed to Assignment 2 by developing our source quality criteria and search strategy. Instead of relying on AI-generated answers, we defined what we considered a reliable source before searching. We then looked for evidence from organisations such as Stanford HAI, WIPO, OECD and European institutions. I helped compare evidence across areas including frontier AI models, research, patents, investment, compute infrastructure and regulation.

One of the most important things I learned was that a question such as **“Who dominates AI?”** does not have one simple answer. The answer changes depending on whether we measure model performance, investment, patents, research, infrastructure or regulation. This made me more aware of the difference between using AI to generate an answer and using AI to analyse evidence that has already been selected and evaluated.

## What did the gates ask, and what did we change?

The feedback process made us look more critically at the quality and recency of our evidence. We identified several areas where our research could be improved. For example, we updated our Stanford evidence to the 2026 AI Index, included newer WIPO patent information, added evidence about compute and infrastructure, and considered official European sources for the EU AI Act.

We also changed the geographical scope of our target audience from marketing agencies specifically in Arnhem and Gelderland to **small marketing and advertising agencies across the Netherlands**. Arnhem can still be relevant for our field research and potential SME partner, but the handbook itself can now be useful to a broader group of Dutch SMEs.

These changes showed me that feedback is not only about correcting mistakes. It can also reveal where the scope, evidence or assumptions of a project are too narrow and need to be reconsidered.

## What did my build role produce?

My work this week contributed to the research process that the AEL platform will eventually need to support. In particular, I worked on defining how sources should be searched for, evaluated and compared before they are used to generate handbook content.

This connects directly to our PRD and technical blueprint because the platform should not simply collect information and ask an AI model to summarise it. It needs to preserve the source, evaluate its quality, keep claims traceable to evidence and allow conflicting information to be identified. The comparison of the three AI-generated articles demonstrated why these checks are necessary.

My main output from this week was therefore not only content for the handbook, but also a clearer understanding of the research workflow that our platform needs to implement. Going into the next week, I want to focus more on how this evidence and source information should be stored and passed between different parts of the platform, while keeping the process understandable for non-technical users.
