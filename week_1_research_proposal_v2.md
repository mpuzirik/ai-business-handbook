# AIBS Research Proposal, Revision 2

Revised after the module-owner questions and the in-class feedback on scope definition and analysis factors. Section 7 lists what changed. Section 8 tracks open questions.

## 1. Scope

The scope is defined in three layers, each with a stated role in the research.

**Sector.** Small marketing and advertising agencies: branding, digital marketing, graphic design, website development, campaigns and content creation for other businesses.

**Size.** Roughly 10 to 50 employees. Large enough to have divided roles and repeatable workflows, small enough that AI adoption is limited by the owner's time, budget and staff skills rather than by an IT department.

**Region.** The Netherlands, with Arnhem and the wider Gelderland region as the sampling region. The region has two defined roles: it gives access to the case company, and it is the level at which we check the innovation and support environment (regional funding schemes, KVK, Oost NL, knowledge institutions such as HAN, and the local labour market for digital and creative skills). Regional findings are reported as regional context, not as sector-wide conclusions. Where no regional evidence exists, we say so and fall back to national data.

**Company.** Reklaame, Arnhem, as the case company for the internal analysis. If Reklaame cannot participate, a comparable agency in the same size and service range is approached.

The business decision the handbook must serve:

**Which parts of a marketing agency's workflow should use generative AI to improve productivity, and which parts should stay primarily human to protect quality, originality and client trust?**

## 2. What These SMEs Need to Know

The Draghi report identifies weak productivity growth, slow technology adoption and dependence on non-European technology providers as core European competitiveness problems. Small agencies sit exactly at that point: generative AI is cheap to try, but almost every capable tool is supplied by a non-EU vendor, and the agency's raw material is confidential client material.

An owner therefore needs to know:

* where AI plausibly creates productivity gains, and how large those gains are;
* which tasks should not be automated, and why;
* what the tools require in skills, data and process maturity, against what the agency actually has;
* which risks need controlling before adoption: accuracy, copyright, privacy, client confidentiality, brand consistency, vendor lock-in and erosion of creative skill;
* what it costs in money and staff time, and how to test small before committing;
* what competitors and front-runners in the sector are already doing.

## 3. Research Question

### Main question

**How can small marketing and advertising agencies in the Netherlands use generative AI to improve productivity while maintaining creative quality, originality and client trust?**

### External analysis

**E1. Complexity and technological limits.** Which generative AI applications are relevant to this sector, what level of technical and organisational complexity does each require, and where do the tools currently fail at agency-grade creative work?

**E2. Autonomy and vendor dependency.** How dependent are these tools on non-EU providers, models and infrastructure? Where is the provider established, where is data processed, and what does that mean for GDPR compliance, copyright position, client confidentiality and the agency's ability to switch or exit?

**E3. Champions.** Who are the front-runners in this sector, in agencies, tools and sector initiatives, and what distinguishes them from the average agency? Are there European alternatives that a Dutch agency can realistically use?

**E4. Competition and client expectations.** How is AI adoption changing pricing, billable-hour models, client expectations and the services agencies sell?

**E5. Innovation environment and financial capacity.** What support exists nationally and in Gelderland for digitalisation in small creative firms (subsidies, vouchers, networks, knowledge partners), and is it accessible to a 10 to 50 person agency?

**E6. HR capacity in the sector.** What is the labour market and skills picture for AI-capable marketing, copy and design staff, and which skills does the sector report lacking?

### Internal analysis (case company)

**I1. Workflow and time.** How does work move from client brief to delivered output, and which activities consume the most employee hours?

**I2. Demonstrated capability and technological limits.** What AI use, data, tooling and infrastructure can the company show in practice, and what blocks adoption technically?

**I3. Financial capacity.** What budget and staff time exist for subscriptions, training and pilots, and how does the company judge return on that spend?

**I4. HR capacity and internal champion.** Who could own AI adoption internally, how much learning time is available, and what is staff attitude to AI-assisted creative work?

**I5. Autonomy and dependency.** How much does the company care where data is processed, what do client contracts and NDAs allow, and how locked in is it to its current tooling?

**I6. Prioritisation.** Which use cases give the best balance of business value, implementation effort, creative quality and risk for this company?

## 4. Quality Criteria for the Handbook

A handbook page must:

* use plain English a non-technical owner can follow;
* answer a business decision, not explain AI technology;
* separate facts, research findings and assumptions, with traceable sources for important claims;
* state opportunities and limits, including privacy, copyright, accuracy and human oversight;
* give examples realistic for a small agency;
* state expected business value, not feature lists;
* not recommend AI where evidence is thin;
* end with concrete actions or questions for the owner;
* stay short enough to be read by a busy owner.

The same criteria are used to judge pages drafted with support from the AEL platform.

## 5. Methodology

### Desk research

Answers the external questions and supplies evidence for the internal analysis. Sources: the Draghi report, European Commission publications, CBS, KVK, Oost NL, sector bodies, academic literature, industry reports, reputable technology and business press, and Dutch-language sources. Each source is appraised on authority, relevance, recency, evidence quality and bias before use.

Three specific tasks are included:

1. **Tool jurisdiction mapping.** For each significant tool: provider establishment, data-processing location, training-data and copyright position, business-tier data handling, and the practical consequence for an agency holding client material. This produces a comparison table for the handbook.
2. **Comparable cases.** 2 to 4 documented cases of similar small agencies that have piloted or adopted generative AI, including failures. These supply practical examples independently of the case company.
3. **Readiness factors.** Desk evidence on sector-level financial capacity, skills shortage and innovation support, used as the benchmark against which the case company is read.

### Field research

One SME visit in weeks 2 to 4, semi-structured interview with an owner, manager or employee who knows the workflows.

Topics: brief-to-delivery workflow; time-heavy activities; current AI use; workflow frustrations; attitudes to AI-generated creative work; copyright, privacy and confidentiality concerns; budget and training time; who would drive adoption internally; contractual constraints on tool choice; which use cases they would test.

AI knowledge is probed through demonstrated recent use, not self-assessment. Instead of "which AI tools do you know", we ask "what did you use AI for recently, and how", and where appropriate ask the participant to describe or show an output. This separates having tried a tool from being able to use it.

### Scope limits

The field research describes one company. It is not used for claims about the sector. Sector-level claims rest on desk research and the comparable-agency cases. Regional observations are labelled regional. The case company is used to see what adoption looks like in practice and to test whether what desk research calls possible is realistic at this size.

## 6. Assumptions and Consequences

| Assumption | If wrong |
|---|---|
| AI can cut time on research, brainstorming, first drafts, brief summaries and asset variants | The productivity premise of the handbook collapses; the page becomes a risk and limits advice instead, still useful but a different deliverable |
| A comparable agency is available if Reklaame drops out | Internal analysis is delayed; fallback is a shorter interview plus heavier reliance on documented cases |
| 2 to 4 documented comparable cases can be found | Examples become generic or vendor-sourced; we would flag them as weaker evidence and lean on the case company |
| Business tiers of major tools allow use with client data | Most identified use cases become non-compliant; recommendations shift to EU-hosted or on-device alternatives |
| The interviewee can speak to both workflow and budget | Financial-capacity questions go unanswered; a second short contact is needed |

## 7. Response to Module-Owner Questions

**1. Is the region substantive or practical?** It was practical in the first draft, and the draft presented it as if it were a variable. It is now a named scope layer with a defined role: access to the case company, plus the level at which innovation support and the skills labour market are checked. Findings about AI adoption itself are framed nationally, because the design does not compare regions. An identically sized Rotterdam agency would probably reach the same conclusions about tools and workflow; what could differ is access to regional support schemes and local hiring, which is exactly what E5 and E6 test.

**2. Does the plan distinguish EU from non-EU tools?** Yes, and this is now a central line of enquiry rather than a risk footnote. E2 and E3 cover provider establishment, data-processing location, copyright position, exit cost and European alternatives. I5 covers what the company's own client contracts permit. This also connects the research to the Draghi report's point about dependency on non-European technology.

**3. Self-reported knowledge versus demonstrated ability?** Sub-question I2 now asks what the company can demonstrate. Interview questions ask for specific recent use and, where appropriate, an example output. One company's answer says nothing reliable about the sector, which is why sector-level capability is answered by E6 and the comparable cases, and why Section 5 states the limit explicitly.

**4. Where do practical examples come from if the case company has barely used AI?** From desk-research task 2, the 2 to 4 documented comparable cases, including unsuccessful ones. These are gathered regardless of what the case company turns out to do, and they double as a comparison point between what research says is possible and what looks realistic at this size.
